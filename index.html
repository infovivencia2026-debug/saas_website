The school

Five offices. One student record.

Admissions, academics, examinations, accounts and campus life each get the screens they actually work in. Underneath, all five are declared in one registry and share the same table engine, drawer and form behaviour — so nothing is entered twice.

The platform

What every office gets for free

The behaviour that makes software usable — searching, sorting, filtering, saving — is written once and inherited, so the fee counter and the exam office work the same way and neither needs its own training day.

One table engine

Search, multi-column sort, filter, pagination, row selection and bulk actions — identical in a fee register, an attendance sheet and a transport roll.

Roles that scope the app

Switching role changes the sidebar, the command palette and the landing screen. A class teacher and the accounts clerk never meet each other's modules.

A registry, not a rebuild

A module is a config entry: typed columns in a compact DSL, or a bespoke view when the tab genuinely needs one.

Desk, tablet or phone

Fixed sidebar and wide tables in the office; a drawer, single-column tiles and stacked cards for a teacher marking attendance on a phone.

Questions

The things people ask first

Is this built for schools specifically?

Yes — only schools. The vocabulary on screen is a school's: classes and sections, terms and report cards, roll numbers and transfer certificates. Nothing has been bent out of a generic business product to fit.

What runs underneath all of it?

Sixteen back-office modules — general ledger, payables, receivables, costing, tax, workforce, attendance, payroll, talent, sourcing, purchasing, orders, analytics, administration, integrations and settings — plus the whole interface layer: tables, drawers, modals, forms, command palette and theming.

Is this a working product or a prototype?

Today it is a front-end prototype. The interface is real and fully clickable — routing, tabs, drawers, modals, search, filter, multi-column sort, pagination, row selection, bulk actions, form validation and drag-and-drop boards all work. The data behind it is generated in the browser, and there is no backend, database or authentication layer yet.

Can it be changed to match how our school works?

A module is usually one registry entry: a list of typed columns written as type:Label@Option,Option. Renaming a field, adding a fee head or changing a grade band is a configuration change, not a rebuild — and it inherits every interface behaviour automatically.

How does role switching work?

Thirteen roles ship with it — class teacher, subject teacher, exam officer, accountant, librarian, warden, transport in-charge, admissions, front office, head of department, vice principal, principal and administrator. The role decides which modules appear and which screen you land on.

Does it work on a phone?

Yes. The office keeps a fixed sidebar and wide tables; a tablet collapses the sidebar and scrolls tables sideways; a phone gets a drawer, single-column tiles and tables rendered as stacked cards — enough for a teacher to mark attendance from the corridor.

What would a real deployment need?

An API and database behind the registry, an authentication and permission layer to enforce what role switching currently only presents, and real integrations — a payment gateway for fees, SMS and email for parents, and biometric or RFID feeds for attendance. The interface layer is the part that exists today.
